YINQI HUANG

CLAd-VR: Multimodal Sensing for Adaptive Training

EEG- and gaze-informed adaptive feedback for VR machining training — NYU Dept. of Computer Science and Engineering.

01. Project Overview

Most adaptive VR training systems reduce a whole training procedure to a single "cognitive load" score. This project asks a more specific question: can physiological signals — EEG, gaze, pupil size, and blinks — tell apart the different kinds of demand that different steps of a hands-on task place on a learner, rather than just how demanding the task is overall? The context is CLAd-VR, an adaptive VR training system for machining skills developed at NYU. My work spans the system's user studies, the Ghost Hands training prototype, and its AI voice guidance layer.

02. Research & Methods

Seven participants completed a full VR machining procedure — 31 logged sub-steps, from securing a workpiece to drilling — on a Varjo XR-3 headset fitted with a 15-channel EEG cap, while PhysioLabXR synchronized gaze, pupil, blink, and EEG streams on a shared clock. We used Multiple Resource Theory, which treats perceptual, cognitive, and motor demand as drawing on largely separate attention resources, to decide in advance which physiological feature should index which kind of demand — then let each sub-step's own recordings determine which demand was dominant.

The Challenge Most evidence linking EEG and gaze signals to cognitive load comes from seated, static lab tasks like mental arithmetic. It was unclear whether those signals behave the same way during a real, physically active, hands-on training task where the learner is constantly moving.

03. Ghost Hands & Adaptive Voice Guidance

Alongside the sensing study, I built Ghost Hands, a Unity/C# training prototype that overlays recorded hand demonstrations, tool interactions, and object highlights directly onto the learner's view, paired with an AI voice guidance layer — a language model selects a coaching strategy given the learner's progress, which is converted to speech and played back in real time.

The Direction Rather than one global difficulty setting, guidance can eventually be triggered per sub-step and informed by which kind of demand — visual, cognitive, or physical — that step places on the learner.

04. Findings & Next Steps

Across the 31 sub-steps, head and eye movement clearly separated motor-heavy sub-steps (like swinging a mallet or loosening a chuck) from visually-focused ones (like locating an edge or reading a digital display) — but no single signal cleanly separated perceptual-dominant from cognitive-dominant sub-steps. We also found that high-frequency EEG activity normally discarded as noise during cleaning tracked head movement, suggesting it isn't pure noise, but carries information about what the learner's body is doing. These findings are preliminary (n=7); the full analysis is written up in a poster submitted to ISMAR 2025 (XRSPro Adjunct). Next steps: extending the study to more participants, and using these per-sub-step demand signatures to actually drive adaptive guidance in Ghost Hands, rather than only describing them.

Co-authored poster: "What Each Step Demands: A Preliminary Multimodal (EEG + Gaze) Characterization of Sub-Steps in VR Machining Training." Xichen He, Bhavya Matam, Yinqi Huang, Kachina Studer, Christian Gabbianelli, John Liu, Claudio Silva, Ziheng Li, Dishita Turakhia. Submitted to ISMAR 2025 (XRSPro Adjunct).
