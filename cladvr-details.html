CLAd-VR: Multimodal Sensing for Adaptive Training

EEG- and gaze-informed adaptive feedback for VR machining training — NYU Dept. of Computer Science and Engineering.

01. Project Overview

CLAd-VR is a team research project at NYU on whether EEG and gaze signals can tell apart different kinds of demand — visual, cognitive, physical — across the steps of a VR training task, instead of giving just one overall "how hard was this" score. It's an adaptive VR training system for machining skills. My part: running the user study sessions, and building the Ghost Hands prototype and the AI voice guidance layer.

02. Research & Methods

Seven participants went through a full VR machining task — 31 logged sub-steps, from securing a workpiece to drilling — on a Varjo XR-3 headset with a 15-channel EEG cap, while PhysioLabXR kept gaze, pupil, blink, and EEG data synced on one clock. We used Multiple Resource Theory (perceptual, cognitive, and motor demand draw on mostly separate attention resources) to decide ahead of time which signal should track which kind of demand, then checked that against what each step's own recordings actually showed. I helped run these sessions — recruiting participants, observing, and doing follow-up interviews.

The Challenge Most evidence linking EEG and gaze to cognitive load comes from seated, static lab tasks like mental arithmetic. It wasn't clear those signals would behave the same way in a real, physically active, hands-on task where the learner keeps moving.

03. Ghost Hands & Adaptive Voice Guidance

Alongside the study, I built Ghost Hands — a Unity/C# prototype that overlays recorded hand demonstrations, tool interactions, and object highlights onto what the learner sees. I paired it with an AI voice guidance layer: a language model picks a coaching strategy based on the learner's progress, turns it into speech, and plays it back in real time.

The Direction Rather than one global difficulty setting, guidance can eventually be triggered per sub-step and informed by which kind of demand — visual, cognitive, or physical — that step places on the learner.

04. Findings & Next Steps

Across the 31 sub-steps, head and eye movement clearly separated motor-heavy sub-steps (like swinging a mallet or loosening a chuck) from visually-focused ones (like locating an edge or reading a digital display) — but no single signal cleanly separated perceptual-dominant from cognitive-dominant sub-steps. We also found that high-frequency EEG activity normally discarded as noise during cleaning tracked head movement, suggesting it isn't pure noise, but carries information about what the learner's body is doing. These findings are preliminary (n=7); the full analysis is written up in a poster submitted to ISMAR 2026 (XRSPro Adjunct). Next steps: extending the study to more participants, and using these per-sub-step demand signatures to actually drive adaptive guidance in Ghost Hands, rather than only describing them.

Co-authored poster: "What Each Step Demands: A Preliminary Multimodal (EEG + Gaze) Characterization of Sub-Steps in VR Machining Training." Xichen He, Bhavya Matam, Yinqi Huang, Kachina Studer, Christian Gabbianelli, John Liu, Claudio Silva, Ziheng Li, Dishita Turakhia. Submitted to ISMAR 2026 (XRSPro Adjunct).
